Research Proposal · DigHum 150B · Due July 31, 2026

Is the World Eating the Same Meal?

The assignment

Draft an evaluation plan explaining the argument of the final project, the intellectual and practical justifications for its choices, and how the work will be evaluated.

The argument

I argue that between 1961 and 2023 national diets did not converge on a neutral global average. They converged on the plate that wealthy Western countries were already eating in 1961, and they did so while the gap in how much people eat stayed roughly as wide as it was. The visualization has to carry both halves of that claim, because either one alone is misleading.

This began as a question I did not know the answer to, which is why I chose it. Food is one of the most durable expressions of culture, and if national diets are becoming interchangeable then something is happening to culture that can be measured rather than merely asserted. The claim above is what the preliminary analysis produced, and it is contestable in three specific places, which is how I have structured the work.

First, has the composition of national diets actually become more similar since 1961, or does it only seem that way because global brands are visible? Second, if diets are converging, are they converging toward an average, or toward a particular existing cuisine? Third, does convergence in what people eat imply convergence in how much they eat, or can diets grow alike while remaining deeply unequal?

Preliminary findings

I ran an initial analysis before writing this proposal, because a research question is only worth proposing if the data can actually answer it. The early results are strong enough that the project is clearly viable, and interesting enough that I want to pursue it.

The world now eats considerably more. Global average supply, taken from the FAO world aggregate, rose from 2,185 calories per person per day in 1961 to 3,006 in 2023, an increase of 38 percent. The growth is not evenly distributed across food types. Oils and fats rose 110 percent, contributing the single largest absolute gain, while meat rose 97 percent and fruits and vegetables rose 141 percent. Two categories fell outright: pulses, meaning beans and lentils, declined 23 percent, and starchy roots declined 7 percent.

Cereals require a more careful statement, because the obvious summary is wrong. Grains did not shrink. They grew 18 percent in absolute terms, from 1,080 to 1,276 calories. What fell was their share of the plate, from 49 percent of all calories in 1961 to 42 percent in 2023. So the accurate claim is not that plant staples are disappearing but that they are being diluted: the world still eats more grain than anything else, while fats and animal products claim a growing portion of a growing total.

On the convergence question, I measured how far each country's dietary composition sits from the global average, then tracked that dispersion across six decades. It falls steadily, by about 25 percent between 1961 and 2023, and it falls in nearly every period rather than fluctuating. National diets really are becoming more alike, and the trend is remarkably smooth.

That result has an obvious confound, which I tested rather than assumed away. The number of countries reporting data grows from 146 in 1961 to 176 in 2023, so a falling dispersion could simply reflect which countries entered the dataset rather than any change in how people eat. Restricting the measurement to the 133 countries present in every single year, convergence is 23.4 percent, against 25.4 percent for all available countries. The finding survives, and it is not an artifact of sample composition.

The direction is the part I found most striking. I constructed a reference profile from what six wealthy Western countries ate in 1961, namely the United States, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Australia, and Canada, chosen because they were already high-income and high-fat at the start of the period and therefore represent a plausible destination rather than a moving target. Measured against the balanced panel, the average distance to that profile falls 30 percent.

A single anchor proves nothing on its own, since general convergence would pull countries closer to any reference point. So I repeated the measurement against three alternative 1961 anchors built the same way. Distance to a South Asian profile rose 15 percent. Distance to an East Asian profile rose 14 percent. Distance to a West African profile was essentially unchanged at 2 percent. Only the Western anchor shows substantial movement toward it. The world did not drift toward everyone at once. It moved toward one plate and away from others, which is what licenses the directional claim.

One finding complicates the story usefully, and I intend to keep it rather than smooth it over. Convergence in composition has not produced equality in quantity. In 2023 the United States averaged 3,947 calories per person per day while Yemen averaged 1,811, a gap of more than two to one. The world's diets are becoming similar in kind while remaining starkly different in amount, and any visualization that shows only convergence would tell a comfortable and misleading story.

Intellectual justification

The pattern I am measuring has a name in public health research. Barry Popkin's work on the nutrition transition describes how societies, as they urbanize and industrialize, shift from diets built on grains and legumes toward diets higher in fats, sugars, and animal products, with consequences that follow for population health. My contribution is not to discover this transition but to visualize it as a question of cultural convergence rather than of nutrition alone, and to ask the directional question that a public health framing tends to leave implicit: convergence toward whose diet.

That reframing is what makes this a humanities project rather than a nutrition one. A cuisine is an inheritance. When pulses fall by a quarter globally, what disappears is not only a protein source but a set of dishes, techniques, and agricultural practices that took centuries to develop. Measuring dietary convergence is a way of measuring cultural homogenization with unusually good data, since food consumption is recorded annually and comparably for almost every country, which is rarely true of cultural practices.

This project also continues a thread from my earlier weekly work. Week 2 charted global literacy rising over two centuries, a story of convergence that is straightforwardly good. This project examines a convergence that is harder to evaluate, since more calories in places that had too few is a genuine gain, while the loss of distinct food cultures is a genuine loss, and the same dataset contains both.

The material

The primary dataset is Our World in Data's dietary composition series, derived from the Food and Agriculture Organization's food balance sheets. It reports daily per capita calorie supply broken into ten commodity groups, including cereals, meat, sugar, oils and fats, pulses, and dairy, for 244 entities from 1961 through 2023. It contains 13,221 rows, far exceeding the hundred-row minimum for quantitative data, and 134 countries have complete records in both the first and final years.

I am using the same source and retrieval method that worked reliably for my Week 2 literacy project. Two different conventions are at work in the numbers above and I want to be explicit about which is which. The global figures, such as the rise from 2,185 to 3,006 calories, come from the FAO's own population-weighted world aggregate, so that a person in China counts the same as a person in Iceland. Every country-level measurement, including all of the convergence and distance results, instead uses unweighted country observations restricted to entities with valid three-letter country codes, with regional aggregates such as Africa and Asia excluded so that no country is counted twice. Mixing the two would be an error, so I am reporting which convention produced which number.

The most important limitation is that food balance sheets measure the supply available for consumption rather than the food people actually eat, so they include waste at the retail and household level. This matters more than a generic caveat suggests, because waste rises with income. Wealthy countries throw away a larger share of what reaches them, which means the two-to-one supply gap between the United States and Yemen overstates the true gap in what is eaten. The direction of that comparison is robust; its magnitude is not. I will therefore label the axis calories supplied rather than calories eaten, and state the asymmetry on the visualization itself rather than in a footnote.

Tools, audience, and design

I will build the project in Flourish, with Tableau as a secondary tool. Both are on the approved list. Flourish is the better fit for the central view because the argument is fundamentally about change over time across many countries, and Flourish's animated and small-multiple formats handle that comparison without requiring a reader to interpret a dense static chart. Tableau will handle the composition breakdowns, where its dashboard linking lets a reader select a country and see its plate change.

My audience is a general reader who eats food and has no background in nutrition science or economics. That assumption drives the design decisions. I will express quantities in intuitive terms wherever possible, showing what a country's plate looks like as proportions rather than as calorie counts, and I will avoid terms like dietary dispersion in favor of showing two plates side by side and letting the reader see them become alike.

The central design challenge is that convergence is a statement about a distribution, which is harder to draw than a trend. My working approach is to show many countries' dietary compositions as small repeated shapes, animated or faceted across time, so that the reader watches them visibly become more similar. The second view will place the convergence trend against the persistent calorie gap, so that the two findings appear together and neither can be read alone.

Scope and feasibility

Two weeks remain, so I want to state what is finishable rather than what would be ideal.

The minimum deliverable is a single Flourish visualization answering the convergence question directly, published and embedded on this site with a short written analysis. This is achievable because the data is already downloaded and the core analysis is already run, as the findings above demonstrate. The remaining work is design and writing rather than discovery.

If time permits, I will add the directional view showing movement toward the 1961 Western profile, and a Tableau dashboard letting a reader explore individual countries, since the country level variation is striking on its own. South Korea's diet changed more than any other country's over this period, while Senegal's changed least, and those two cases would make the aggregate trend concrete.

This project is also a natural candidate for continuing after the course, since joining dietary convergence to income, urbanization, or trade data would let me ask why convergence happened rather than only whether it did. That requires more time than two weeks allows, so I note it as a future direction rather than proposing it now.

How I will evaluate this work

I want criteria specific enough that I could fail them, so I am stating them in advance.

  • The caption test

    The visualization must convey its finding through title and labels alone. I will show it to at least three readers with the surrounding text hidden and ask what they conclude. If they cannot state that diets are becoming more alike, the design has failed and I will change the encoding rather than add explanatory paragraphs.

  • Every claim must survive a second method

    My convergence result uses mean Euclidean distance from the global average composition. Before publishing I will recompute it as mean pairwise cosine distance between country profiles, which measures similarity of shape rather than of magnitude. If the direction of the trend flips, I will report both rather than present whichever looks better. I have already applied this standard twice: the convergence finding was retested on a balanced panel of the 133 countries present in every year, and the directional finding was retested against three alternative anchors. Both survived, and had they not, this section would say so.

  • Convergence must not be shown as equality

    The calorie gap between the richest and poorest countries must appear alongside the convergence finding. A visualization implying that the world now eats alike, when the United States consumes more than twice what Yemen does, would be a distortion even though every underlying number was accurate.

  • No causal language

    This data can establish that diets converged and in which direction. It cannot establish why. I will not use words like caused, drove, or led to, and if a reviewer finds causal implication in my phrasing I will rewrite it.

  • Reproducibility

    The dataset and the scripts that generate every figure will be published in this site's repository so that any number in the final writeup can be regenerated from source.

On process, I will keep a decision log recording each analytical choice and its cost. I began this practice in Week 5, where I restricted a correspondence network to pairs exchanging ten or more letters, which improved legibility but removed one-time correspondents, and I stated that tradeoff on the page rather than hiding it. Here the equivalent choices are which countries to include, which reference profile defines the Western plate, and how to treat missing years, and each will be recorded with what it excludes.

The strongest evidence of success would be a viewer who comes away able to answer the question I started with, including the uncomfortable half of the answer. If the visualization convinces someone that global diets are converging but leaves them believing this means the world now eats equally well, it will have failed regardless of how accurate its numbers are.
