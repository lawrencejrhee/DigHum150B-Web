From "I" to "We": The Evolution of Inaugural Rhetoric

The assignment

Choose one data visualization tool you learn in the week and do a visual analysis on a humanities topic you like (broadly construed). This week I am working with Voyant Tools.

Tool
Voyant Tools
Topic
Pronoun use in US presidential inaugural addresses
Corpus
All 60 addresses, 1789 to 2025

Why Voyant this week

Voyant Tools is a text analysis environment that runs in the browser. A user uploads a document or a collection of documents, and Voyant produces a set of linked visualizations from them, including word clouds, frequency tables, collocation views, and trend lines across a corpus. Nothing is installed and no code is written, which makes it a practical tool for working with literary and historical texts.

I chose Voyant over the other tools covered this week because my question is about language rather than about numbers. Datawrapper and Infogram expect a table of values as their input. Voyant expects the text itself, which means the analysis can begin from the documents rather than from a spreadsheet that someone has already summarized. For a question about how presidents write, that distinction matters.

The specific feature I am relying on is the Trends panel. Voyant orders the documents in a corpus and plots the relative frequency of chosen terms across that sequence. Because the inaugural addresses are dated and evenly spaced through American history, the document sequence is also a timeline, and a frequency trend across documents becomes a historical argument.

The corpus, and why all of it

The corpus is the complete set of United States presidential inaugural addresses, from George Washington in 1789 through 2025. That is 60 addresses and roughly 141,000 words. The texts come from the C-Span inaugural address corpus compiled by Kathleen Ahrens and distributed with the Natural Language Toolkit, and each address is stored as its own file named by year so that Voyant reads them in chronological order.

The decision to use every address rather than a selection is deliberate. A sample invites the question of why those particular documents were chosen, and any pattern found in a sample can be an artifact of the selection rather than a property of the tradition. Because the inaugural address is a fixed and continuous genre, the full population is small enough to analyze completely. Whatever trend appears cannot be explained by which speeches were included, because none were left out.

Why pronouns rather than content words

The second choice is which words to track, and this one requires more justification. It would be natural to search for words such as war, economy, or freedom. The difficulty is that content words move with events. The word war becomes frequent when there is a war, and its rise and fall describes American history rather than American rhetoric. A chart of content words mostly tells us what was happening at the time, which is something we already know from other sources.

Function words behave differently. Pronouns such as I, my, we, and our appear in every address regardless of subject matter, because they are part of the grammar of speaking rather than part of any particular topic. This is the same reasoning that leads stylometric research to use function words for authorship attribution: they are largely independent of what a text is about, so variation in them reflects how the writer is positioned rather than what the writer is discussing.

That is what makes the comparison worth charting. If first person plural pronouns rise while first person singular pronouns stay flat, the explanation cannot simply be that the topics changed. It suggests that the speaker's relationship to the audience changed, from a president describing his own intentions to a president speaking on behalf of a collective.

The analysis

Counting the corpus directly, at a rate of occurrences per 10,000 words, the pattern is clear. In the addresses from 1789 to 1828 the word we appears about 40 times per 10,000 words. In the addresses from 1989 onward it appears about 277 times, an increase of roughly seven times. Over the same period the word I barely moves, going from about 74 to about 68. The plural pronoun our follows we upward, from about 118 to about 268.

A second pattern appears alongside it. Average sentence length falls steadily across the corpus, from about 62 words per sentence in Washington's first address to about 17 in the most recent ones. The inaugural address becomes both more collective and more plainly spoken over the same two centuries, which suggests these are two aspects of a single change in how presidents address the public.

To test whether the pronoun trend is really about rhetoric rather than about circumstances, the chart below also tracks the content word war. It behaves exactly as a content word should. Instead of a trend it produces isolated spikes: Madison in 1813 at about 124 occurrences per 10,000 words, Lincoln in 1865 at about 171, Harding in 1921 at about 42, and Roosevelt in 1945 at about 36. Between those peaks it sits near zero.

The more interesting detail is where the spikes are absent. Lincoln's first inaugural in 1861 uses war only about 6 times per 10,000 words, and Roosevelt's third inaugural in 1941 does not use the word at all. These are the two most famous moments of national foreboding in American history, and in both cases the inaugural address is nearly silent about war. The reason is chronological: inaugurals are delivered in January or March, and in both cases the war had not yet begun. Fort Sumter was still weeks away in 1861, and Pearl Harbor was still more than ten months away in 1941. The spikes appear in 1865 and 1945 instead, when each war was ending.

This is what makes the control useful. The word war does not simply track events, it tracks the position of the speech relative to those events, which shows that the inaugural address is a retrospective genre rather than a predictive one. A content word measures circumstance that precisely. The pronouns do something different: we climbs steadily across the same two centuries without regard to which wars were being fought, which is the behavior of a word that reflects stance rather than subject.

First reflections on the tool

Voyant is unusually fast for a first pass at a corpus. Uploading 60 documents and getting a set of linked views takes about a minute, and the Trends panel answers a question about word use across two centuries almost immediately. For exploratory work that speed is valuable, because it makes it cheap to abandon a question that turns out to be uninteresting.

The limitation is that Voyant counts words without understanding them. It does not distinguish the we that means the administration from the we that means the nation, and both are counted the same way. It also treats every document as an equal unit even though the addresses range from 135 words to more than 8,000. The tool can show that a pattern exists, but deciding what the pattern means is still an interpretive act that belongs to the reader rather than to the software. That is a version of the same tension I noticed with Tableau in Week 2, and it seems to be a general feature of these tools rather than a quirk of any one of them.
